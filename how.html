What this is and how it works

A quick read of SoCal flying for the next 7 days: one score per site and day, so you see which day and which site deserve a closer look.

Optimistic on purpose: it shows how good a day can be. Little Black, Blossom, Elsinore and Torrey are tuned against pilots' usual weather tools and local reads; the other sites are educated guesses. Always check the real forecast and conditions on launch.

How the score is made

Every site and day gets one number from 1 to 5, built in the same steps every time. Open a site and How the score was made shows these steps with their points.

  1. Start from the height. The top of usable lift over the launch and the hills around it (maps below) sets the starting number. Each site has three marks: good local height, XC height and great XC height.

    In between it goes up smoothly. Lift above XC height with climbs under 1 m/s starts at 3.4 at most: high but too weak to go far.
  2. Shave it hour by hour. Each hour keeps that number only if the hour is good to fly: climb (1 m/s gives about half credit, 2 m/s or more full), launch wind, gusts, wind at the top of lift, offshore flow and storms each take their share.
  3. Take the best 3 hours between 10 am and glass-off. Glass-off, the last 3 hours before sunset, is rated on its own and can lift a day to 3.
  4. Points off for the day, the same at every site, when they hold through most of the flying window:

  5. Limits last. "2 at most" and the like are applied after all the points off, so a limit is never counted twice.
  6. The verdict. 3.5 or more is XC (with the site's XC height, workable wind and at least 1 m/s climb); a 5 needs great height and nothing taken off.

An example: Little Black with lift to 2,800 ft starts a bit above 4. Good climbs and a west wind well inside the window keep it there, an LAX–DAG of −3 mb takes 1 off: a 3 for the day, "Site fly", even though the height says XC.

The windy signal warns of a steady breeze most of the window (10 mph on launch or 13 at the top); it takes no points. When only some models show a step, the step names them (for example "ICON: E 12 mph").

Sites with their own rules

Torrey: wind decides. A forecast 6 mph from WSW–WNW is soarable, 4–6 marginal, under 4 too light, over 20 too windy. A good ridge-soaring day is a 4, a light or cross one about 3, a thermal day a 5. Rain only counts when it's likely.

Elsinore: needs east wind or calm, then the west switch. The card says when it switches, or "no switch".

Laguna / Horse / Fuzz: rates above 2 only when the convergence is there. Horse can also glass off in a light W–SW evening breeze; Laguna can't. The card shows the better launch.

Trip areas (experimental): Santa Barbara / Ojai and Owens; the best launch counts. Owens is for big, calm days only: over 10 mph at the top of lift or 8 on launch makes it 2 at most.

Models

The first 2–3 days use HRRR and RRFS, then RRFS with ECMWF, GFS and ICON. Days 5–7 run on global models only, so expect them to change. The bottom of each day lists the models and their run time.

Optimistic on purpose: a global model more than a point below the best other model at a site is left out of that site's day, and on global-only days the best two count. One model doing something odd doesn't sink a day.

Santa Ana comes from two signs: offshore (northeast to east) wind at about 5,000 ft, and the LAX–DAG pressure gradient, the sea-level pressure at LAX minus Barstow-Daggett. About −2 mb is weak offshore, −4 mb a real Santa Ana, −6 mb strong. Each model's gradient is checked against the two airports' actual readings over the last three days and corrected, because coarse models put Daggett at the wrong height.

The morning balloon (sounding)

Every morning around 4 am a weather balloon goes up from Miramar. It measures the real air, not a model. Edwards, Vandenberg, Yuma and Las Vegas are one tap away (Edwards and Yuma only when they fly and share it).

  • Thermal tops: how high a thermal goes for a given ground temperature, and for today's forecast high.
  • Cloudbase and inversions (the blue bands that cap the lift).
  • Launch heights of the nearby sites as dashed lines, so you see at a glance whether the lift clears them.
  • The balloon's path on a little map, with the last few launches in grey.

What it doesn't know

Live observations, local effects, turbulence and rotor, overdevelopment, closures, permits and airspace. Always check the real forecast and conditions on launch.

Where the radar measures

Wind is read at the launch. Height is also read at points around it, because the best climbs are often over the hills behind launch.
